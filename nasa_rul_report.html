NASA PHM Asymmetric Scoring Formula
Late predictions penalized more aggressively than early ones
PHM Score (per engine):
• If e < 0 (early prediction): exp(−e / 13) − 1
• If e ≥ 0 (late prediction): exp(e / 10) − 1

where e = predicted_RUL − true_RUL

Total PHM Score = sum across all engines. Lower is better.

Key asymmetry: Late predictions (denominator=10) are penalized ~30% more steeply than early predictions (denominator=13). A model that consistently predicts conservatively (early) benefits from this asymmetry — which is exactly what XGBoost does on this dataset.

1

sensor_11 is the Dominant RUL Predictor

With |Pearson|=0.696 and |Spearman|=0.718, sensor_11 is the strongest individual predictor of Remaining Useful Life. Its value increases monotonically across the lifecycle of every single training engine — a perfectly reliable degradation signal that forms the backbone of the feature set.

2

6 Constant Sensors Carry Zero Information

sensor_1, sensor_5, sensor_10, sensor_16, sensor_18, sensor_19 output constant values across all engines and all cycles in the FD001 dataset. Including them would add dimensionality without predictive value. They are excluded before all feature engineering steps.

3

XGBoost Achieves Test MAE of 10.37 Cycles

The final XGBoost model predicts turbofan RUL with a test MAE of 10.37 cycles and RMSE of 14.30 cycles on the 100-engine test set. Approximately 50% of engines are predicted within 10 cycles of true RUL, and ~80% within 25 cycles. This represents accurate and operationally useful predictions for maintenance scheduling.

4

NASA PHM Score of 309.72 — Conservative Model Favored by Asymmetric Scoring

The NASA PHM score of 309.72 on the test set reflects a competitive result. The model's tendency to predict RUL conservatively (early) is actually favorable under the PHM scoring function, which penalizes late predictions ~30% more steeply than early ones (denominator 10 vs 13).

5

282 Features from 21 Raw Sensors via Temporal Engineering

The feature engineering stage transforms 21 raw sensors into 282 features using rolling windows of size 5, 10, and 20 cycles: rolling mean, rolling std, rolling min, rolling max, exponential moving average, and linear trend slope. This rich temporal representation captures degradation rate, not just instantaneous sensor values.

6

Engine-Level GroupShuffleSplit Prevents Data Leakage

Row-level random splitting would allow data from the same engine to appear in both train and validation sets, creating severe optimistic bias. The engine-level GroupShuffleSplit ensures completely disjoint engine sets between train and validation, producing a realistic estimate of out-of-sample performance on unseen engines.
